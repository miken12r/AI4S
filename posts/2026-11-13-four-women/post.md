## STORY

On March 2, 1955, 15-year-old Claudette Colvin refused to give up her
seat on a Montgomery bus and was dragged off and arrested. In 1956 she
was one of four women who sued the city over its bus segregation laws,
and on November 13, 1956, the Supreme Court affirmed their win.

## POST TEXT

"I just couldn't move. History had me glued to this seat."
— Claudette Colvin, on her arrest in Montgomery, 1955

On March 2, 1955, months before Rosa Parks, 15-year-old Claudette Colvin refused to give her bus seat to a white passenger. Police dragged her off. "I paid my fare," she told them, "and it's my constitutional right."

The next year she was one of four women who sued the city over its bus segregation laws, with Aurelia Browder, Susie McDonald and Mary Louise Smith. A fifth plaintiff, Jeanetta Reese, withdrew after threats against her and her family. Colvin testified in federal court.

Seventy years ago today, the Supreme Court affirmed their win in a single paragraph. The Montgomery bus boycott ended five weeks later.

Colvin died in January, at 86. For years her name was left out of the story. She said that didn't bother her, "as long as we have someone out there so we can tell our story."

## REPOST COMMENT

What stays with me is how young two of them were. In the court record, Claudette Colvin and Mary Louise Smith each sue through an adult "next friend," because they were minors. The other side's lawyers included the Alabama attorney general; the plaintiffs had Fred Gray, Robert Carter, and, at the Supreme Court, Thurgood Marshall.
